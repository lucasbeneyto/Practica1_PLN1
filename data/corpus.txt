Language models estimate the probability of a sequence of words, and that
simple idea turns out to be enough to write poetry, answer questions, translate
sentences, and carry on a conversation. A model does not store sentences: it
stores statistical habits. Given everything it has read, it learns which token
is likely to follow another, and generation is nothing more than repeated
sampling from that distribution. The quality of a model is therefore measured
less by the size of its memory than by the shape of the patterns it can
reproduce, and by how gracefully it handles a sentence it has never seen.

The transformer architecture changed the field because it replaced sequential
computation with attention. Every position can look at every previous position
in a single operation, which makes training highly parallel and lets the model
route information across long distances. A causal mask keeps the generative
property intact: each token may read the past but never the future. Around that
core the standard recipe adds residual connections, layer normalization, and a
small feed-forward network that is applied to each position independently.

A practical problem appears as soon as sequences grow: the model must know
where each token sits. Early implementations learned a vector for every
absolute position and added it to the token embedding, a scheme that works well
up to the length seen during training and then simply has no parameters for
longer inputs. Rotary embeddings take a different route. Instead of adding a
position vector, they rotate the query and key vectors by an angle proportional
to their index, so the dot product between two positions depends only on how
far apart they are.

That relative view has consequences that are easy to demonstrate. Shift an
entire window of text a few positions to the right and the attention scores of
a rotary model do not change at all, while the scores of an absolute model
change with every shift. The rotary model also carries no positional
parameters, which removes a small amount of memory and makes the scheme
attractive for long contexts. None of this comes for free: a model pretrained
with absolute positions cannot be converted by simply discarding the learned
embeddings, because the rest of the weights were optimized around them.

Evaluating such choices requires more than a single number. Perplexity on held
out text measures how surprised a model is by what it reads, but it hides the
structure of the failure: a model can be fluent yet lose track of order, or
remain faithful to the structure while sounding repetitive. It is therefore
useful to combine a perplexity measurement with controlled synthetic tasks, in
which the dependency to be learned is known exactly and the only variable is
the length of the sequence the model has to handle at test time.

Reproducibility is the quiet foundation of all of this. Fixed seeds, a versioned
corpus, a documented configuration, and a clear separation between the reusable
library and the experimental notebook make it possible to rerun an experiment
months later and obtain the same table. Good scientific code is not clever
code: it is code that makes the comparison honest, that reports the numbers it
actually measured, and that fails loudly when an assumption stops holding.

Once the pieces are in place, the interesting part begins: watching the two
models disagree. On short sequences they are almost interchangeable, and any
difference is lost in the noise of initialization. As the context stretches
beyond the training length, one of them stays steady while the other begins to
hallucinate order, and the plot of loss against length becomes the real
argument for or against the change. That is the experiment this notebook is
built to run, and it is worth running carefully.
